You know what needs to get done. The laptop is open, the document is blinking at you, and you have the time. Yet you cannot make yourself start.

If you have ADHD, autism, or experience executive dysfunction, you probably know this wall well. You have also almost certainly heard the advice to try **body doubling**: working alongside another person so their quiet presence helps you get over the starting line.

For many people, body doubling feels almost magical. Suddenly the emails get answered, the taxes get sorted, or the chapter gets written.

Then you try a live body doubling platform, such as a Zoom room, a Discord voice channel, or a 1-on-1 Focusmate session, and something unexpected happens. Instead of feeling calmer, your shoulders climb up to your ears.

## Why body doubling works in the first place

Body doubling is not about being supervised or forced to perform. At its core, it works because human nervous systems are deeply social, even when we are working on solitary tasks.

When you sit alone with an unstructured or boring task, your brain has to generate all of the initiation energy from scratch. When someone else is quietly focused in the same space, three subtle shifts happen:

* **Shared momentum:** Seeing someone else already in motion acts as an external cue. Your brain does not have to decide *whether* it is time to work; the environment already signals it.
* **Gentle co-regulation:** A calm, steady presence next to you can lower the internal friction that fuels task paralysis and spiraling thoughts.
* **A soft container for time:** Knowing that a quiet block of shared focus has started makes the task feel bounded and manageable, rather than an endless void.

The trouble is that traditional body doubling assumes *another live human on camera* is the only way to create that container.

## When live body doubling backfires

For introverted, autistic, sensory-sensitive, or socially anxious people, and especially for anyone dealing with Pathological Demand Avoidance (PDA), live body doubling sessions often introduce the exact stress they were meant to relieve.

Think about everything a 50-minute video call quietly demands before you even open your task:

* **Scheduling ahead of time:** You have to predict, hours in advance, when your brain will have the capacity to focus. If that moment arrives and you are already depleted, cancelling feels awful.
* **Small talk and check-ins:** Explaining your goals to a stranger, smiling on camera, and reporting what you finished at the end burns precious executive energy before work even begins.
* **Being perceived:** Having a webcam pointed at your face turns a private workspace into a stage. Many neurodivergent people spend half the session masking: monitoring their posture, their facial expression, or whether they look "productive enough."
* **Fear of interrupting or failing:** If you need to stand up, pace around the room, answer the door, or stop after twelve minutes because your focus ran out, you worry about letting someone down.

> When the tool meant to reduce friction requires a calendar invite, a clean background, and eye contact on Zoom, the tool itself becomes another task to avoid.

If you have ever signed up for a body doubling session and felt relieved when your partner cancelled, there is nothing wrong with you. Your nervous system was simply reacting to performance pressure disguised as support.

## The case for asynchronous, camera-free presence

Once you separate the *mechanism* of body doubling from the *social performance* of a video call, an interesting question appears:

Do you actually need someone watching you, or do you just need calm, steady company beside you?

For many people, asynchronous presence, where a companion is simply there working quietly in their own world without expecting anything back, delivers the anchor without the social cost:

* **Zero scheduling:** You can start the moment a spark of energy appears, even at 11:30 p.m. on a Tuesday.
* **Zero masking:** You can wear pajamas, sit cross-legged on the floor, fidget, mutter to yourself, or stare at the ceiling while thinking. Nobody is watching.
* **Zero social debt:** There is no greeting to manage, no goal to justify, and no awkward goodbye when you only had fifteen minutes in you.
* **Complete predictability:** The atmosphere stays calm and steady every single time you open it.

You still get the visual cue of someone quietly focused beside you. You still get a gentle boundary that says, *this is our quiet focus time now*. You just leave the webcam, the microphone, and the social fatigue out of the room.

## How I built this into neben

This exact frustration is why I built [neben](/en/neben) (*neben* is the German word for *beside*).

The idea is deliberately simple: you place your phone beside your laptop, your notebook, or whatever is in front of you. On the screen, a calm illustrated animal companion, such as a fox in a rainy attic cabin or a capybara in a quiet night train, sits at a warm, lamplit desk and quietly reads, writes, and turns pages alongside you.

There is no camera, no microphone, no account, and no stranger waiting on the other end. Everything runs 100% offline on your device.

Because real life happens while you work, **neben** never punishes you for stepping away. If you pick up your phone to grab a two-factor code or answer a quick message, a 90-second grace period keeps your session running while the rain gently dims. If you need a longer break, your companion simply slips a bookmark into the book and waits calmly until you are ready to continue.

At quiet intervals, your companion also models small breaks, like taking a silent sip from a warm mug, stretching, or resting their eyes, without pop-ups, alarms, or chewing noises. You can follow along, ignore it, or switch any nudge off.

You do not need an audience to get started. Sometimes, all it takes is a quiet desk beside yours, and someone who is simply there while you begin.
